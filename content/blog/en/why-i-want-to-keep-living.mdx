I was scared of dying from very early on. I was eight or nine, not yet in third grade. One night I was sleeping in the same bed as my grandpa, and I asked him what happens after you die. He seemed to really not want to talk about it. He said, "Hey, don't say unlucky things," and told me to go to sleep.

Back then, lying under the covers, it would suddenly hit me that one day I'd just be gone, the world would still be there, and I'd never know what happened next. The more I thought about it the more scared I got. But a kid can't do much about that, so I'd put it down and go back to sleep.

Later I even seriously thought about secretly researching how to live longer, finding some way to live a very long time without hurting anyone.

As I grew up the thought came back less and less. Every day there were classes, exams, projects, and being alive just felt like the default. I'd wake up tomorrow, I'd still be here next year, and I didn't really think about it.

---

These days I'm more sure than before that I'll live a long time, mostly because of AI. I think research is going to keep speeding up because of it, and I largely believe that within my lifetime, human lifespans will get a lot longer. If that's true, and I really do live that long, I'm probably still in the childhood of my life.

So that fear from when I was little has faded a lot.

A while ago I was talking with an AI about the future, and at some point we got onto living a very long time. It's very good at picking holes; every round it found a new question, and we drifted further and further. Below are a few of the questions it asked me, and what I said.

## "Wouldn't living that long get boring?"

I don't really believe it would. Living a long time doesn't mean repeating today a hundred million times. The world changes, technology changes, I change, and I'd keep running into new problems, new people, and things I can't even describe now. If I actually get there and I'm bored, I'll deal with it then. First, stay alive.

## "A hundred million years from now, would that still be you?"

By then I'd probably have forgotten most of what's happening now, my body might have been replaced several times, and my personality and views would have changed. It asked what exactly I want to carry on: my memories, my body, my personality?

I thought about it, and the answer is pretty simple: I want to be alive. I'm here now, I can see, feel and think about things, and I don't want that to stop. Whether the person a hundred million years from now counts as me doesn't have to be decided today.

If I'm alive, everything can still change. I can get things wrong, regret them, choose again, and it's fine even if one day I reject everything I am now. If I'm dead there's nothing, and no chance to change my mind either.

## "You must need some reason, though?"

I used to think wanting to live a long time needed a big reason, like unfinished work, or wanting to see humans leave the solar system. Of course I'd like to see those things. But even without them I'd still want to live. Wanting to know what happens tomorrow is enough.

My mom and I both think it's better to live on badly than to die well.

## "But some people say death is what gives life meaning."

I can see what that means: time is limited, so people treasure certain moments more. But I don't think it follows that death is a good thing. That saying feels like looking at a whole life from the outside and liking that it has a beginning and an end. But I'm living inside it, and for me, death just means I'm gone.

## "What about people who don't want to live that long?"

That's up to them. Some people can face death calmly, some think a normal lifetime is enough. I won't decide for them, and I don't want anyone deciding for me.

What I hope for is that people who want to keep living aren't forced to stop by an illness that could have been treated, or by an ageing that could have been fixed.

---

When I think back to that night now, I still don't know what happens after you die. But I'm not as scared anymore. More than that, I want to know what happens next.
