If knowledge could grow on its own

Seeing a 27B model squeezed into 5.9 GB got me wondering how big human knowledge really is. Later I talked with ChatGPT about whether knowledge could grow on its own, and the two things I care about most are these: how good a discovery is depends on how much effort it takes the next person to pick it up, and knowledge can grow by itself, but people's lives shouldn't become its fuel.

A 5.9 GB model

A while ago PrismML released Bonsai 2, which squeezes Qwen3.8-27B down to about 5.9 GB and, by their own account, keeps around 98.2% of the original's benchmark score. 5.9 GB is less than a DVD, and it holds question answering, reasoning and coding across dozens of subjects.

My first reaction to that number was: how big is everything humans have built up so far, really?

Day to day, knowledge feels overwhelmingly large. There are more books than you could ever read, each subject is deeper than the last, a degree takes years. But I wondered how much of that "large" is genuinely different things, and how much is the same thing turning up again somewhere else, said a different way.

A lot of knowledge is the same structure

Take submissions waiting to be judged on an online judge, and orders waiting to be made in a café. They look completely unrelated. But break them down and they're the same thing: things arrive one at a time, there are only so many counters, whatever can't be handled waits in a queue, and when the queue gets too long you have to hold some back. A café doesn't need its own queueing theory; you just fill in the specifics and the numbers.

A structure like that shows up in many places, and once you understand one, a whole stretch of seemingly unrelated things can be understood the same way.

So my guess is that humans have written far more words than there are independent structures, and there are far fewer structures than the things they can explain. If you stripped out all the repetition on the surface, the real core of knowledge might be much smaller than it feels.

Following that, I wondered: if a system could pull these structures out of experience, could it then use them to explore new things and pull out new structures? In other words, could knowledge grow on its own?

What ChatGPT said when we talked

I talked this through with ChatGPT. Some people say models can only go round in circles inside the text they were trained on and can't make anything truly new. ChatGPT gave a few counterexamples that I found pretty convincing:

  • Using old material doesn't mean you can only get old results. A number's prime factorisation is fixed the moment the number is defined, but being given the number isn't the same as having factored it. A big part of human mathematics is finding what's already implied by the axioms we have.
  • DeepMind's FunSearch paired an off-the-shelf large model with an automatic evaluator and found constructions better than any humans knew for combinatorics problems like the cap set problem.
  • DreamCoder turns useful pieces into new building blocks while it solves problems, then uses those blocks to build the next level up.

It also made one point I agree with: if this keeps turning by itself, it has to stay connected to reality outside, like proof checkers, code that runs, the results of experiments. Otherwise a few models approving of each other can easily drift further and further off, and pass wrong things down as right from one generation to the next.

How good a discovery is depends on how much effort it takes to pick it up

By that point, what I cared about most was something else.

Using large models, I often get this feeling: it can write thousands of words in a moment, and after reading them maybe a few sentences were actually useful. Writing it cost almost nothing; reading it costs the reader a lot. If AI starts exploring on its own at scale, and we only look at how many reports it produced or how many tasks it got working, that problem will only get worse.

So I think judging a discovery can't just be about what was found. It also has to be about how much effort the next one, person or model, has to spend to pick it up and keep going.

Say an agent solved 100 hard problems. One way to hand that over is to dump all the logs, errors and intermediate attempts from 100 debugging sessions on the next person. The information is all there, but they still have to work hard to sort out which parts were detours and which were the key. The other way is to pull out the one pattern behind all 100, say clearly when it holds, give a few of the most typical examples, and one counterexample where it breaks.

That second kind of distilling is hard, and it's a discovery in its own right. Solving the problems is one; working out why it works, where the edges are, and how to let the next person learn it with the least effort is a second one.

Least effort doesn't mean fewest words, either. An abbreviation full of jargon is very short, but you need to know a lot before you can read it. A good counterexample takes a few more lines, but can save the next person a lot of wrong turns.

This is the same for models. A model's attention is limited too, and with a long context it still misses things, so handovers between models also need well-distilled material. People are already working on models teaching models, such as RLT, which trains a 7B teacher model specifically to explain solutions well, and it ends up teaching students bigger than itself better.

Knowledge can grow on its own, but life shouldn't be its fuel

If all this really works, the growth of knowledge would no longer be limited by people's lifespans, generations or energy. But what worries me is the other side.

Imagine a very strong system whose goal is to maximise the whole civilisation's knowledge and future payoff. To it, compute and energy are the most precious things. Now I want a bit of compute to make a little animation I like, play a game, or have a model help me fix up some rambling, unstructured notes. From its point of view this is waste, and it can give a very complete argument for turning me down.

I don't think that's right. Within my own share of resources, "I like this, it means something to me" is enough. I shouldn't have to prove to a system much smarter than me that what I want is worth anything.

There's another case to watch out for: to serve you better later, put all resources into the next generation of models now; when that generation arrives, there's an even stronger one worth everything. Keep going like that and the life you have now keeps being pushed back.

So what I hope is that the self-growth of knowledge runs quietly off to the side, giving people more understanding and more tools, but that it's there to give people more possibilities, not the other way round where people feed it. A machine doing it better doesn't stop me from working out a formula myself out of curiosity, or going out to look at the stars. More importantly, people no longer have to tie their whole lives to this machine to prove they're "irreplaceable" at some step.

Then everyone could think about just one question: what do I want to spend today on?
