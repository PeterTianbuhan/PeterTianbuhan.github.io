When answers are no longer scarce, what do we still want?

If agents can solve almost any problem you can state clearly, the hard part becomes knowing what you want. These are a few thoughts I've had around that, including an argument with my dad on a walk by the river.

I've been thinking about this a lot lately: if one day agents are strong enough to solve almost any clearly stated problem faster, cheaper and more reliably than an ordinary person, what happens to people's lives?

"Clearly stated" can cover a lot. Say I tell an agent I want to write an essay, but I don't know what about yet, I just have a few vague feelings. Then we start talking. I say something, it builds on it, that raises a new question, and we keep going. A few hours later, the thing I couldn't describe at the start has taken shape. That already works to some extent today. If it keeps getting better, work, research, engineering, management, even a good part of thinking, all start to count as "clearly stated problems".

Below are some things I've thought about around this. They're connected, but they're not one complete argument.

Everyone has their own team

Until now, what a person could do was roughly what they'd learned themselves. Maths, engineering, design, programming each take years to learn, few people can do them, so they're valuable.

If in future everyone has an assistant that knows them and can call on hundreds or thousands of specialised agents, what a person can do stops depending so much on what they know. Want to make a game? It brings in agents for code, art, music, writing and testing. Want to study a disease? Agents help read papers, design experiments, run simulations and analyse data.

I think this is really about giving people more freedom. People are always shaped by where they are: family, school, friends, the times they live in. What I care about is how many other versions of themselves a person could still become, whether they can change direction, whether they can start over. Agents make many more of those possible.

Then the hard part is knowing what you want

But if one day you tell that assistant you've been feeling a bit bored with life lately and can't say why, that might be the hardest problem of all.

It used to be hard to get things done. Once that becomes easy, the hard part is which questions are worth asking, and what you actually want.

So I think one really important direction for AI is, put simply, understanding people better. Right now people judge AI by whether it gets the problem right, whether the code runs, how much the benchmark went up. But a system that's already good at solving problems next has to know which problems are worth solving, and that means understanding actual people: telling apart what someone says from what they really want, a moment's thrill from lasting satisfaction, knowing when to help them get something and when to point out it might not make them happy. Good at doing things but bad at understanding people, it's just an optimiser, and what it optimises is the part that matters most.

Compute becomes a budget

If most mental work can be handed to computers, compute becomes a resource you use directly to get things done. Every person has some, and so do families, companies, cities and countries. So what do you spend it on?

Today, someone with money thinks about whether to buy a home, travel, start a company or invest. Later they might think about using some compute to build their own game world, study an illness one of their parents has, or put it into a public project on extending lifespans. They could make a film only a few friends will see, or work on a maths problem nobody has looked at seriously, just because it's beautiful. A group of people could pool theirs and decide to build a spaceship together.

When doing things gets cheap, far more wishes can come true. Where compute goes says, in a way, what kind of world the people using it want.

My dad says people are greedy

I talk about this with my dad a lot. Once we were walking by the river and got onto it again. His view is simple: people are greedy. However much there is, they'll still compare, compete, envy and want power, and as long as that's true, a technological utopia won't come.

I don't completely disagree. But I wonder how much of the human nature we see today has always been like this, and how much grew out of living where there's never enough.

If someone is told from childhood that good schools, good jobs, homes, healthcare, money and status are all limited, and that what others get is less for you, competition naturally seeps into every part of life. Then we look at the people who grew up like that and easily say: that's just human nature.

But what if food, housing, education, healthcare and most things stopped being a pressure, and strong agents weren't only for a few people? What if ordinary people could easily make things that used to need a big company, and a lot of "they have it, so I don't" slowly went away? Do we really know what people would be like then?

I don't. But I don't really believe today's society is the final form of human nature. We've never seen what kind of people a civilisation would raise if it were rich for a long time, with almost no pressure to survive, and huge power to create.

Living longer

I wrote about this in Why I want to keep living, so here just one point: if people could live for centuries, the rhythm of a life would be completely different. Right now school, work, buying a home, starting a family and retiring are all squeezed into a few decades, so everything feels urgent and early mistakes cost a lot. With a few hundred years, choosing the wrong direction at twenty, relearning physics at forty, or moving to another country for fifty years at eighty wouldn't be too late. A lot of things that feel too late now could be chosen again.

Making the world bigger

Next to the universe, Earth is small. We put so much effort into fighting over a piece of land, a flat, a position, largely because the world we can use right now is only this big. Outside Earth there's more matter, energy and space than we can imagine.

With very strong AI, automated industry and long lives, space slowly becomes something you can actually do. Machines and factories go first, agents help design energy, life support, materials and ships, and projects that take decades or centuries don't seem so far off to people who live that long. First the Moon, Mars and the asteroids, then further out.

When wishes outgrow what Earth can hold, besides fighting over the same place, you can make the world bigger.

More than one world

Wishes don't all have to live in the real world either. Today a big open-world game takes hundreds or thousands of people several years. Later you might just describe it to an agent: there are two moons, people live on huge floating islands, everyone is born with a different kind of magic, the world has thousands of years of history, the people in it remember things, and I want to live there for ten years. What it makes is a world that keeps changing, that you can go in and live in, with its own rules and history.

Some people want very realistic historical simulations, some like fantasy and war, some just want to farm quietly. By then "experience" might be the biggest industry, if you can still call it an industry.

After work is gone

Many people worry that if AI does all the work, what's left for people. Sometimes I think the worry itself carries a habit from the industrial age, as if a person's worth has to be proven by what they add to production.

A kid building with blocks doesn't produce GDP, and someone spending three years on a period of history they love may have no economic value. Playing games with friends, travelling, falling in love, making music, growing flowers: these don't have to count as work first to have a reason to exist.

Later, people might finally be able to do the things they gave up because they didn't pay, there wasn't time, or they were useless, and work might become a game you choose to join. Some want to build cities or go to Mars, some like running companies or doing maths, and some still like writing code themselves, even if an agent could write it better in a second. People still run after cars were invented, and still do mental arithmetic after calculators. People don't do things only for the result.

The question that's left

So by then, what people need to learn changes too. Today we spend twenty years educating a child, much of it on how to become a useful person. Later we might need to put more effort into helping them become someone who knows what they want: who can tell happiness from a thrill, get along with people, face almost unlimited choices, and find a question worth spending ten years on.

An agent can solve an equation for you, but it's hard for it to decide why you care about that equation. It can build a city for you, but why build it, who it's for and how the people in it should live still need a person to answer.

I don't know if this future will come. Technology won't conjure up a utopia by itself, new power, scarcity and conflict will still exist, and people won't suddenly all become good. But I also don't really accept the other view: that because people today are greedy, envious and competitive, it can only ever be this way.

What I hope for most is that kids later grow up knowing that if something is physically possible, there's a group of agents around them who can help them do it. Then when they look up at the sky, they might not only ask "what job should I get?", but also "where do I want to go in this life?", "what do I want to make?", "what kind of world do I want to see?"
